# Silicon Valley Bank 2023 Bank Run

- ID: `CASE.SVB.2023_BANK_RUN`
- Domain: META / Systems Thinking
- Date studied: 2026-09-10
- Primary concept: Feedback Loop
- Status: Studied; Teach-back pending

## Puzzle

Why did Silicon Valley Bank's March 8 attempt to strengthen liquidity and capital contribute to a run that closed the bank two days later?

## Before

SVB tripled in size from 2019 to 2021 as technology and venture-capital clients deposited funding proceeds. It invested a large share of those deposits in longer-duration securities. At year-end 2022, roughly 94% of deposits were uninsured, and securities represented about 55% of assets compared with about 25% for peer large banking organizations.

## Pressure Builds

Rising interest rates reduced the market value of long-duration fixed-rate securities. At the same time, weaker venture funding led clients to consume deposits for operations. The bank therefore faced declining deposit stability while its assets were costly to liquidate without recognizing losses.

## Turning Point

On March 8, SVB completed the sale of securities with about $23.97 billion in book value for about $21.45 billion and recognized an after-tax loss of approximately $1.8 billion. It also announced a planned $2.25 billion capital raise. Depositors interpreted the action as a distress signal.

## Cascade

Concern prompted uninsured depositors to transfer funds. Withdrawals reduced liquidity and increased the need for financing, collateral, asset sales, or rescue. Those visible signs of stress increased concern and prompted more withdrawals. A concentrated technology and venture-capital network plus digital banking accelerated the return path.

## Outcome

- Approximately $42 billion left on March 9, close to one quarter of the deposit base used in the supervisory review.
- More than $100 billion in additional withdrawals were expected for March 10.
- California regulators closed SVB on March 10 and appointed the FDIC as receiver.
- Authorities announced full depositor protection on March 12 to limit broader systemic-risk transmission.

## Comparison And Evidence Boundary

FDIC transaction-level research released in 2026 found that large uninsured depositors were much more likely to run, while fully insured retail depositors generally did not run before failure. This supports an incentive-based mechanism rather than a claim that social media affected every depositor equally. Public aggregate evidence cannot prove which specific message caused any individual transfer.

## Counterfactual

More stable insured funding, shorter-duration assets, effective interest-rate hedging, tested emergency borrowing capacity, slower withdrawal technology, or an earlier credible guarantee could have weakened different links. No single intervention erases the underlying risk-management failures.
