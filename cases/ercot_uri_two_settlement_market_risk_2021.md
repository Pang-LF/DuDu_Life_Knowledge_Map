# ERCOT Winter Storm Uri: Two-Settlement Market Risk, 2021

- Case ID: `CASE.ERCOT.URI.TWO_SETTLEMENT_MARKET_RISK.2021`
- Domain: SYSTEM / Electricity Markets
- Primary concept: Day-Ahead, Real-Time and Two-Settlement
- Status: Studied; Teach-back pending

## Puzzle

How could ERCOT's annual average day-ahead and real-time prices finish relatively close while individual retailers, generators, and counterparties experienced catastrophic cash-flow losses?

## Before

ERCOT's day-ahead market allowed QSEs to create next-day financial energy positions and manage real-time price and congestion risk through day-ahead purchases and sales, bilateral contracts, owned generation, and other hedges. Real-time settlement then priced the difference between scheduled and metered quantities.

## Pressure Builds

Winter Storm Uri simultaneously increased electric and heating demand and impaired generation equipment, natural-gas production, processing, and fuel delivery. The same weather factor could increase a participant's uncovered physical quantity while also increasing the real-time price used to settle that quantity.

## Turning Point

ERCOT entered Energy Emergency Alert 3 early on February 15. The 2021 State of the Market report records more than 52 GW of generation unavailable at the event peak. Participants relying on failed owned generation or contracts could become real-time buyers even when they appeared hedged beforehand.

## Cascade

Real-time prices exceeded $1,000/MWh for 166 hours. More than 64 hours reached $9,000/MWh, with roughly another 34 hours between $7,000 and $8,999/MWh. The annual average real-time price was $167.88/MWh, but only $40.73/MWh after excluding Uri; day-ahead prices averaged about $157/MWh. Aggregate averages therefore concealed sharply different interval-level volumes and positions.

## Decision

Load-serving entities had to choose day-ahead coverage, bilateral and owned-generation hedges, demand response, customer pricing, and collateral. Generators had to choose offered quantities, fuel and winterization arrangements, and how much buy-back exposure to accept if physical output failed. More day-ahead coverage reduced one spread exposure but could not eliminate location basis, performance, collateral, or counterparty risk.

## Outcome

The market monitor reported short payments exceeding $3 billion. Several retail electric providers exited and a large cooperative entered bankruptcy proceedings. These outcomes cannot be attributed to forecast error alone; outages, fuel failures, emergency pricing duration, contract terms, ancillary services, collateral, and default allocation also mattered.

## Aftermath

Texas used securitization and market reforms to address financial losses and future reliability. The system-wide offer cap was later reduced from the event-era $9,000/MWh to $5,000/MWh effective in 2022. The historical cap is used here to reconstruct the 2021 event, not as a statement of permanent or universal market rules.

## Causal Judgment and Evidence Boundary

The report supports the chronology, price magnitudes, outage scale, and settlement stress. It supports the interpretation that two-settlement rules concentrated marginal deviation costs on short positions while protecting covered day-ahead quantities at their day-ahead price. It does not identify a clean causal share for forecast error or prove that any single hedge, market reform, or physical investment would have prevented defaults.

## Transfer

A hedge is never just a price. It has a quantity, interval, location, counterparty, performance condition, and credit requirement. Similar market-wide averages can coexist with insolvency when losses are concentrated in the wrong participant at the wrong time.
