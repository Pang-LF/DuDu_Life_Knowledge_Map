# Fairchild Planar Process, 1957-1961

- Case ID: `CASE.FAIRCHILD.PLANAR_PROCESS.1957_1961`
- Domain: SYSTEM / Compute Systems and BUSINESS / Semiconductor Industry
- Primary concept: Chip Fabrication Foundation
- Industry application: Foundry and Fab Economics

## Puzzle

Why did leaving a glass-like oxide on a silicon surface solve a reliability problem and also create a scalable manufacturing language for integrated circuits?

## Minimum Unit And System Outcome

The minimum unit is one patterned opening in silicon dioxide that selects where a later diffusion, contact or material change may occur. The system outcome is a repeatable processed wafer containing many aligned devices and interconnects, not one successful laboratory transistor.

## Narrative Structure

- Before: Fairchild initially produced mesa transistors whose raised geometry left junction edges vulnerable to surface contamination and leakage.
- Pressure Builds: The company needed reliable high-frequency silicon devices for demanding customers, while conventional practice removed the oxide after it had served as a diffusion mask.
- Turning Point: Jean Hoerni formalized the planar idea in January 1959 and demonstrated a working device in March, retaining protective oxide over junction edges while opening controlled windows for later processing.
- Cascade: Oxide served as mask, coordinate surface and permanent protection; repeated openings enabled nested doped regions and same-side contacts; Robert Noyce then used the insulated surface to envision patterned metal interconnections among multiple devices on one wafer.
- Decision: Fairchild shifted production toward the planar process, commercialized the 2N1613 in April 1960 and licensed the method, accepting process redevelopment and yield-ramp work in exchange for reliability and scalability.
- Outcome: Planar devices showed lower leakage and enabled same-side manufacturing. Fairchild became a major high-performance silicon transistor producer and used the platform for early commercial integrated circuits.
- Aftermath: Modern materials, transistor architectures and tools have changed, but fabrication still repeats film formation, lithographic selection, pattern transfer or electrical modification, cleaning and measurement across many aligned layers.

## Integrated Mechanism

Lithography creates a temporary positional pattern. Etching, diffusion or implantation and deposition convert selected positions into persistent material or electrical differences. The retained oxide protected sensitive junctions and offered an insulated plane for later contacts and interconnects. Process integration made the output of each operation a controlled input for the next, allowing laboratory devices to become repeatable wafer-level manufacturing.

## Numbers That Change The Interpretation

- Hoerni recorded the idea in December 1957, filed the related patent in May 1959 and Fairchild commercially introduced a planar transistor in April 1960; the sequence shows that concept, prototype, patent and production were separate states.
- NIST describes modern wafer fabrication at the order of 40-100 repeated pattern cycles, 40-70 masks and up to roughly 2,000 total steps; these are not a universal recipe but show how much integration lies behind one processed wafer.
- In a toy independent-opportunity calculation, 99.9% success repeated 100 times gives about 90.5%, while 99.5% repeated 100 times gives about 60.6%; real yield is not independent, but repeated exposure magnifies local stability differences.

## Comparison And Evidence Boundary

Mesa and planar transistors could both function, but the planar structure protected surface junctions and enabled same-side patterned processing. Historical evidence supports the planar process as foundational; it does not justify assigning sole causal credit for all later IC progress, which also depended on materials, lithography, etch, interconnect, cleanroom, metrology, design and packaging advances. Modern NIST step counts are order-of-magnitude descriptions and vary by product and process.
