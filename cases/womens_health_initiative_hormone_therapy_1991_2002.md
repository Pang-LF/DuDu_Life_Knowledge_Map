# Women's Health Initiative and Menopausal Hormone Therapy, 1991-2002

- Case ID: `CASE.WHI.HORMONE_THERAPY.1991_2002`
- Domain: META / Causal Reasoning; exposure to Clinical Epidemiology and Women's Health
- Primary concept: Confounder
- Status: Studied; Teach-back pending

## Puzzle

Why did observational evidence associate menopausal hormone therapy with substantially lower coronary risk while a randomized trial of a specific combined regimen did not support cardiac prevention?

## Minimum Unit

One postmenopausal woman's treatment decision. Age, symptoms, baseline disease, smoking, healthcare access, clinician selection, and health behavior can influence both whether treatment begins and later coronary risk.

## Narrative Structure

- Before: Hormone therapy relieved symptoms, changed plausible cardiovascular biomarkers, and was studied through long observational cohorts.
- Pressure Builds: The 1991 Nurses' Health Study followed 48,470 women for 337,854 person-years and reported an adjusted relative risk of 0.56 for major coronary disease among current estrogen users.
- Turning Point: WHI randomly assigned 16,608 women with a uterus to a specific estrogen-plus-progestin regimen or placebo, reducing systematic baseline confounding by treatment selection.
- Cascade: The trial stopped after a mean 5.2 years when the global risk-benefit balance crossed its safety boundary; final coronary hazard ratio was 1.24 and was highest in the first year.
- Decision: The evidence no longer supported the studied regimen for prevention of coronary disease, while clinical decisions still had to distinguish symptom treatment, age, timing, formulation, and contraindications.
- Outcome: The original global index estimated 19 excess events per 10,000 person-years across specified benefits and harms.
- Aftermath: Practice changed, while later work refined rather than erased the importance of population, formulation, initiation timing, and individualized symptom treatment.

## Confounding Mechanism

A pre-treatment variable confounds the comparison when it changes both treatment probability and outcome risk. Healthy-user selection and prescribing decisions can make treated and untreated groups differ before the first dose. Adjustment helps only for adequately measured common causes represented with a justified model; unmeasured, mismeasured, or misspecified factors leave residual confounding.

## Numbers That Change The Interpretation

- 0.56 is a relative risk in an adjusted observational model, not a 44 percentage-point absolute reduction or proof of treatment effect.
- WHI randomized 8,506 women to the studied regimen and 8,102 to placebo, making baseline common causes less likely to determine assignment systematically.
- The final coronary hazard ratio of 1.24 compares event rates over follow-up; it does not add 24 percentage points to every person's risk.
- Nineteen excess global-index events per 10,000 person-years is about 0.19 percentage points per year in that composite, with benefits and harms occurring in different disease categories.

## Evidence Boundary

Confounding is an important candidate explanation for observational protection, but the Nurses' Health Study and WHI did not test perfectly identical formulations, age distributions, initiation timing, prior use, or adherence patterns. The case supports careful comparison of assignment mechanisms, not the claim that all discrepancy was caused by one healthy-user variable or that all hormone therapy has one effect for every population and purpose.
