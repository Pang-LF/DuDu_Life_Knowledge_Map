# Smoking and Lung Cancer Causal Inference, 1950-2004

- Case ID: `CASE.SMOKING_LUNG_CANCER.CAUSAL_INFERENCE_1950_2004`
- Domain: META / Causal Reasoning; exposure to Epidemiology and Public Health
- Primary concept: Correlation, Causation, and Reverse Causality
- Status: Studied; Teach-back pending

## Puzzle

How could researchers conclude that smoking causes lung cancer without ethically randomizing people to smoke for decades?

## Minimum Unit

One British doctor's smoking status recorded in 1951 before a later disease or death outcome. Prospective measurement prevents a later diagnosis from changing the original exposure record.

## Narrative Structure

- Before: Lung cancer was rising while roads, exhaust, occupations, pollution, infection, and smoking were competing explanations.
- Pressure Builds: The 1950 case-control study found only 0.3% non-smokers among 649 male lung-cancer patients versus 4.2% among 649 male controls, but retrospective exposure measurement left direction and other alternatives open.
- Turning Point: The 1951 British Doctors Study recorded exposure before outcome in 34,439 male doctors, sharply weakening the claim that later cancer created the earlier smoking record.
- Cascade: Prospective order, dose and duration gradients, cessation patterns, repetition across studies, population timing, and biological mechanisms increasingly constrained reverse-direction explanations.
- Decision: The 1964 Surgeon General's committee reviewed more than 7,000 articles and distinguished causal, probable, associated, and insufficient-evidence conclusions by disease.
- Outcome: The committee concluded cigarette smoking caused male lung cancer; the 50-year cohort later found continuing smokers died about 10 years younger on average.
- Aftermath: Warning labels, advertising restrictions, cessation programs, and later evidence treated exposure reduction as an intervention while preserving uncertainty about individual attribution.

## Numbers That Change The Interpretation

- 0.3% versus 4.2% non-smokers in matched male case and control groups makes chance an implausible explanation for the observed association, but does not identify direction by itself.
- 34,439 doctors followed prospectively establish exposure before later outcomes at scale.
- For men born in the 1920s, middle-age death probabilities were about 43% for continuing smokers and 15% for non-smokers; these are all-cause mortality probabilities, not lung-cancer incidence.
- Cessation near ages 60, 50, 40, and 30 gained about 3, 6, 9, and 10 years of life expectancy relative to continuing smoking; these are group survival differences after sustained cessation.

## Comparison And Evidence Boundary

An ashtray or yellow fingers can predict smoke exposure but removing the marker while preserving smoking does not remove the causal exposure. Illness-induced quitting is different: the outcome changes the exposure label, creating reverse causality. The evidence strongly supports smoking as a population cause of lung cancer but cannot identify with certainty whether one individual's cancer would have occurred without smoking.
