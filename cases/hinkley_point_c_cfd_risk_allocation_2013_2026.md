# Hinkley Point C CfD and Risk Allocation, 2013-2026

## Case ID

`CASE.HINKLEY_POINT_C.CFD.RISK_ALLOCATION.2013_2026`

## Anchor Concept

`BUS.ENERGY.LONG_TERM_CONTRACTS_COST_OF_CAPITAL`

## Before

The United Kingdom sought 3.2-3.3 GW of new nuclear capacity from two EPR units. The project required most capital years before generation and would recover it over a multi-decade operating life. Uncontracted wholesale electricity revenue could not give lenders and equity investors the same debt-service visibility as a durable contracted cash flow.

## Pressure Builds

The first UK EPR project combined design localization, licensing, supply-chain, construction, schedule, currency, and long-run market-price risk. Leaving every risk with the project would preserve full private loss exposure but raise required returns, reduce debt capacity, and increase the revenue needed for investment.

## Turning Point

The government and project entered a 35-year two-way Contract for Difference. The original strike price was GBP 92.50/MWh in 2012 prices, indexed under the contract. The Sizewell C condition later reduced the price by GBP 3/MWh to GBP 89.50/MWh. The project sells electricity into the market; the contract settles the difference between the reference price and strike price in both directions.

## Cascade

The contract narrowed post-commissioning wholesale-price risk and transferred low-reference-price exposure to electricity customers, while customers benefit when reference prices exceed the strike price. Gain-share, refinancing, legal-change, output, termination, and commissioning provisions created a more granular allocation than a simple public-versus-private label.

## Decision

Principal construction cost and delay risk remained with investors rather than being automatically passed through to consumers. This preserved cost-control incentives but required investors to price construction tail risk. The National Audit Office later argued that alternative approaches accepting more public construction risk might have reduced financing cost and should have been compared more fully.

## Outcome

EDF's 2026 annual-results materials organized the project around Unit 1 operation in 2030 and estimated GBP 35 billion completion cost in 2015 sterling under its stated convention, with another GBP 1 billion possible for a further twelve-month delay. EDF also disclosed that additional financing needs exceeded the shareholders' original commitment, that voluntary additional equity had been used since Q3 2023 without CGN participation, and that EDF had signed up to GBP 4.5 billion of Apollo debt financing.

## Aftermath

The project shows both sides of contracting. A long CfD can create bankable price visibility for capital-intensive low-carbon generation, but it cannot stabilize risks outside its clauses. Consumers receive high-price protection yet carry low-price and long-horizon technology-choice exposure; investors retain construction, schedule, performance, and residual financing exposure.

## Evidence Boundary

- Contract duration, strike-price mechanism, indexation, and legal protections are documented facts from UK government contract materials.
- EDF schedule, completion-cost, equity, and debt-financing figures are company disclosures, not independent causal estimates.
- Historic construction-cost figures use different price bases, interest treatment, exchange conventions, and reporting scope. They establish a material upward revision but are not used to calculate one clean overrun percentage.
- The case does not identify the CfD's causal reduction in WACC or prove that another financing model would have lowered total customer cost.
- The NAO's counterfactual critique supports further comparison; it does not by itself establish the outcome of a government-financed, RAB, merchant, or alternative-technology project.
