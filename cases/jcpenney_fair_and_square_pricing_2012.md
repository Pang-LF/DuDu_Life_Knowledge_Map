# J.C. Penney Fair and Square Pricing, 2012

- Case ID: `CASE.JCPENNEY.FAIR_AND_SQUARE.2012`
- Domain: META / Cognitive Bias & Source Evaluation
- Primary concept: Anchoring
- Status: Studied; Teach-back pending

## Question

Why could simpler lower everyday prices weaken customer value perception when a promotional reference price disappeared?

## Narrative

J.C. Penney shifted in 2012 from a promotional department-store model toward lower everyday prices and specialty-store presentation. The previous high-low system showed a higher reference price and a discounted transaction price; the new system reduced that visible comparison. The company later acknowledged difficulty communicating its value proposition and reintroduced some promotions.

## Quantitative Anchors

- Net sales fell from $17.260 billion in 2011 to $12.985 billion in 2012, a $4.275 billion or 24.8% decline.
- Comparable-store sales fell 25.2%.
- Internet sales fell 33.0% to $1.023 billion.
- Gross margin fell from 36.0% to 31.3%, a 4.7 percentage-point decline.
- Sales per gross square foot fell from $154 to $116, about 24.7%.

## Mechanism Evidence

Tversky and Kahneman's 1974 controlled experiment used a wheel preset to 10 or 65 before participants estimated the share of African countries in the United Nations. Median estimates were 25% and 45%. Because the initial number was arbitrary and group exposure differed, the experiment identifies a general anchoring mechanism more cleanly than the company case.

## Evidence Boundary

J.C. Penney changed promotion, merchandising, brands, store presentation, advertising and execution together. The company-wide before-and-after comparison therefore cannot estimate anchoring's independent causal effect. The case supports ecological plausibility and business consequences; the controlled experiment supports the mechanism. Neither proves that all customers use reference prices identically or that high-low pricing is always superior.
