# California Solar Duck Curve, Curtailment and System Value, 2013-2024

## Case ID

`CASE.CALIFORNIA.SOLAR.DUCK_CURVE.SYSTEM_VALUE.2013_2024`

## Anchor Concept

`BUS.ENERGY.LEVELIZED_COST_SYSTEM_VALUE`

## Before

When solar penetration was low, an additional midday MWh commonly displaced fuel-burning generation. Falling module costs and zero fuel expense made LCOE a useful first screen, but the value of output still depended on what the grid would otherwise have run.

## Pressure Builds

Solar resources share a strongly correlated daily shape. As more capacity entered, midday net load fell while the need for other resources shifted toward sunset. CAISO's early duck-curve work warned that a spring day could combine midday overgeneration with an approximately 13,000 MW three-hour evening ramp by 2020.

## Turning Point

Once thermal minimums, exports, transmission, storage and flexible demand could not absorb all available output, CAISO had to curtail renewable generation. Its monthly data show combined wind-and-solar curtailment increasing from about 51,129 MWh in 2014 to 3,423,376 MWh in 2024.

## Cascade

Curtailment did not mean California had enough capability in every later hour. CAISO's August 2024 report recorded maximum solar output of 19,650 MW, about 71,570 MWh of wind-and-solar curtailment, and a maximum three-hour net-load ramp of 18,119 MW. Surplus midday MWh did not automatically become dispatchable evening MW.

## Decision

The system could accept some curtailment, add batteries, expand regional transfers, shift demand, diversify resource shapes, or retain flexible generation. Each option addressed a different constraint and brought its own capital, duration, efficiency, network, emissions or behavioral cost. Zero curtailment was not automatically the least-cost objective.

## Outcome

By May 31, 2024, grid-scale battery capacity in the CAISO balancing area had reached 9,163 MW, more than double the 4,515 MW reported one year earlier. Batteries increased the ability to absorb midday output and serve evening ramps, while gas and imports still contributed materially to ramping and reliability.

## Aftermath

The case separates a resource's average lifecycle cost from its marginal system value. More of the same low-cost output shape can reduce the next unit's energy and capacity value, while complementary storage, transmission and flexible demand can raise it again. Portfolio value must therefore be recalculated rather than attached permanently to a technology.

## Evidence Boundary

- CAISO curtailment totals are absolute MWh, not a curtailment rate; installed renewable capacity, demand, imports, storage, transmission and reporting conditions changed over the period.
- The 13,000 MW ramp was a planning scenario, while 18,119 MW was an observed monthly maximum reported for August 2024; they are not a clean forecast-versus-outcome test.
- Battery growth and curtailment movement are contemporaneous facts, not proof that batteries alone caused any particular reduction or reliability outcome.
- EIA LCOE and LACE are model outputs under explicit financing, utilization, policy and regional assumptions, not universal prices or a complete social-welfare ranking.
