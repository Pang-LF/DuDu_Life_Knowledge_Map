# Texas CREZ Congestion and Locational Value, 2005-2014

- ID: `CASE.TEXAS.CREZ.CONGESTION_LOCATIONAL_VALUE.2005_2014`
- Domain: SYSTEM / BUSINESS
- First studied: 2026-10-21
- Primary concepts: `SYSTEM.ELECTRICITY.CONGESTION_LOCATIONAL_VALUE`, `BUS.ENERGY.WHOLESALE_MARKET_LOCATIONAL_PRICING`
- Status: Case studied; Teach-back pending

## Puzzle

Why could an additional MWh of West Texas wind have a negative same-hour price while one more MWh consumed elsewhere in ERCOT still required positive-priced generation?

## Before

West Texas had strong wind resources far from major load centers. New wind farms could be built faster than the high-voltage corridors needed to export all of their output during windy hours.

## Pressure Builds

The West-to-North transfer constraint bound in 3,121 fifteen-minute intervals in 2009. The West load-zone hourly average price was below zero for more than 700 hours, while zonal wind curtailment was about 442,000 MWh and local curtailment exceeded 3.4 million MWh under the report's different accounting definitions.

## Turning Point

When the export limit bound, the next West-side MWh could not displace the next expensive generator serving distant load. Security-constrained dispatch had to reduce West generation and increase generation on the importing side. The marginal system cost therefore depended on location.

ERCOT's nodal market began on 1 December 2010 and calculated locational marginal prices at more than 8,000 nodes. The market did not create the physical constraint; it made the marginal cost of respecting it more granular and visible.

## Cascade

Federal production tax credits, renewable-energy credits, and some plants' operating constraints could make negative offers rational. When the network could not export all available wind, those offers pushed West prices below zero. Elsewhere, load still needed generation that could physically reach it, so prices did not need to be negative at the same time.

The price spread changed settlement outcomes and incentives for redispatch, new generation, storage, flexible demand, and transmission. It did not by itself identify who should own a project or recover its full fixed cost.

## Decision

Texas designated Competitive Renewable Energy Zones and selected a transmission scenario expected to accommodate 18,456 MW of renewable capacity. The 2008 optimization study estimated Scenario 2 at $4.93 billion, including about 2,334 miles of new 345 kV right-of-way, and expected average annual wind curtailment of 2.31% under its assumptions.

## Outcome

The Public Utility Commission of Texas reported that final CREZ upgrades were completed in early 2014, adding more than 3,600 miles of transmission at a reported cost of $6.9 billion. The different $4.93 billion and $6.9 billion figures should not be treated as a clean cost-overrun percentage because they refer to different planning and final-scope records.

ERCOT's 2013 market monitor reported that the major West-to-North constraint was no longer significant by year-end and that actual wind generation was about 99% of available wind, versus about 96% in 2012 and 92% in 2011.

## Aftermath

Congestion did not disappear from Texas. Local Odessa and later load-growth constraints remained or emerged, illustrating that transmission expansion changes the network's binding constraint rather than abolishing scarcity. New generators, load, retirements, and weather can move the next bottleneck.

## Causal Judgment

The evidence strongly supports transmission expansion as a major contributor to reduced West Texas export congestion. It does not isolate CREZ as the only cause: the nodal-market transition, weather, load, other upgrades, and generation changes also affected curtailment and prices.

## Evidence Boundary

- The 571 negative-price hours reported for the West zone in 2012 are hourly load-zone averages, not every node or every five-minute interval.
- Negative price means an extra local MWh had negative marginal value under then-current offers and constraints; it does not mean electricity had no value everywhere.
- Locational marginal price is a marginal operating signal. It does not, by itself, recover every generator's or transmission project's fixed cost.
- Congestion revenue is a market-settlement quantity and is not automatically transmission-owner profit.

## Transfer

The same logic appears wherever a shared network has location-specific bottlenecks: cloud regions, ports, gas pipelines, warehouse aisles, and urban roads. The relevant question is not merely whether capacity exists, but whether one more unit can cross the binding path at that time.
