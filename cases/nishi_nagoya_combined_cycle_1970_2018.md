# CASE.NISHI_NAGOYA.COMBINED_CYCLE.1970_2018

## Case

Nishi-Nagoya Thermal Power Station Group No. 7（西名古屋火力发电站 7 号机组）, Japan, 1970–2018.

## Anchor Question

How can hot exhaust generate a second round of electricity, and why can no heat engine recover all input energy as work?

## Before

The station began commercial operation with oil-fired capacity in 1970. Units 1–4 eventually aged into a system built for an earlier demand, fuel, and technology context.

## Pressure Builds

Fuel consumption, carbon dioxide emissions, aging equipment, and the economic value of each additional megawatthour increased the case for replacing the old units. A simple gas turbine could provide flexible power but would release substantial high-temperature exhaust energy.

## Turning Point

Chubu Electric decided in 2010 to decommission Units 1–4 and develop LNG-fired Group No. 7. Each block combined three gas turbines with one steam turbine. Heat-recovery steam generators used gas-turbine exhaust to raise steam for an additional power cycle.

## Cascade

Recovering exhaust heat reduced fuel input per unit of electricity but added steam turbines, HRSGs, condensers, pumps, cooling and water systems, controls, maintenance disciplines, capital, and operational coupling.

## Decision

The operator accepted more fixed assets and system complexity in exchange for lower fuel use and emissions over a high-utilization plant life. The choice was a capital-for-fuel trade-off, not an efficiency contest detached from operating hours.

## Outcome

Unit 7-1 entered commercial operation on September 29, 2017. It had 1,188.2 MW of output, and Unit 7-2 brought the group to 2,376.4 MW. Chubu Electric and Toshiba Energy Systems reported 63.08% generation efficiency on a lower-heating-value basis in 2018, recognized then as a combined-cycle world record.

## Aftermath

The case makes Heat Rate, LHV versus HHV, gross versus net output, and actual utilization essential to any efficiency comparison. It also explains why lower-efficiency simple-cycle turbines persist: rapid, infrequent peak service may value flexibility and low fixed capital more than annual fuel savings.

## Quantities and Meaning

- 63.08% LHV: about 63.08 units of reported electric output per 100 units of LHV fuel energy at the stated generation boundary; it is not directly comparable to HHV or net figures.
- 1,188.2 MW per block and 2,376.4 MW for Group No. 7: rated power, not annual energy production.
- Three gas turbines plus one steam turbine per block: the physical structure that converts high-temperature exhaust into additional power.
- 500,000 tonnes LNG and 1.4 million tonnes CO2 annual reductions: project estimates reported by Chubu Electric for the two-block replacement, not independently identified causal effects.
- EIA 2020 averages of 7,146 versus about 10,000 Btu/kWh for combined versus simple cycle: industry context under a different accounting and operating boundary, not a direct comparison to 63.08% LHV.

## Evidence Boundary

Plant configuration, capacity, commercial dates, and the record efficiency come from the operator and equipment supplier. EIA fleet data independently support the general heat-rate difference between combined and simple cycle but do not re-test this Japanese plant. Design or acceptance efficiency should not be treated as every-hour performance, total-system cost, or lifecycle emissions.
